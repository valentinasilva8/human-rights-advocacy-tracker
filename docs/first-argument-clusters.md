# First argument clusters

**Status: proposed on 9 October 2026. Not approved. Not loaded into the demo database.** A person still has to check each passage against the PDF. The five labels in the [annotation guide](annotation-guide.md) still need legal-mentor review.

These notes come from reading six fairness reports. Full PDFs are not in this repository. Page numbers are the printed pages. A short quotation is kept so a reviewer can find the sentence. It is not a republication of the report.

## How to read a row

The wording is "the report argues," not "the restriction violates." Each report says its views are not necessarily those of the Clooney Foundation for Justice. ABA-drafted reports also say they are not the position of the American Bar Association. A named expert's grade is that expert's fairness grade of the trial. It is not an argument, a sentence, or a later outcome.

The five labels overlap. One argument can carry more than one. Anything outside them is `unmapped—review required`, including legitimate aim, insult laws on their face, assembly, discrimination, improper motive, and fair-trial procedure.

Events below are what the report itself states. They are single-source until the judgment or a later document is read. A report published after a verdict does not explain that verdict. An acquittal, a strike-out, a suspended sentence, and a release are different events. None of them is acceptance of the report's argument. Nothing after each report's publication date is known from these files.

Shared sentences are a drafting pattern. Three Cambodia reports using the same Article 495 paragraph are one pattern applied to three cases, not three independent authorities.

## Cluster A — Cambodia, Criminal Code Article 495

**Cases:** Cambodia v. Kong Raiya (November 2020), Cambodia v. Ros Sokhet (February 2021), Cambodia v. Kak Sovannchhay (May 2022).

**Who argues:** Staff of the American Bar Association Center for Human Rights helped draft these reports, as an implementing partner. Arthur Traldi (Kong Raiya) and Alex Conte (Sovannchhay) are the named TrialWatch Experts Panel authors. Sokhet's grade of D was assigned by ABA Center staff who sit on that panel. The expression analysis is a partner draft in all three, read together with the named expert where one is listed. It is not an institutional Clooney Foundation determination. Each grade is D.

Article 495 is described as criminalizing direct incitement to commit a felony or to disrupt social order (Sovannchhay's report uses "disturb social security"). The facial argument is nearly the same in all three.

### A1. The offense is not precise enough

**Labels:** Legality, Vagueness.

The reports argue that Article 495 is insufficiently precise because "social order" is undefined, the provision covers every felony and any disruption of that undefined order, and the resulting discretion is ripe for abuse. They cite Human Rights Committee General Comment No. 34, paragraph 25. Sokhet, printed p. 20. Sovannchhay, printed pp. 37–38. Kong Raiya, printed p. 23.

Sovannchhay's report adds that the Human Rights Committee in 2015 told Cambodia to avoid vague terminology and overly broad restrictions (CCPR/KHM/CO/2, para. 21(d)), and that the Working Group on Arbitrary Detention in 2021 found that Article 495 fails to distinguish violent acts from peaceful exercise of fundamental freedoms. Printed p. 38.

### A2. The offense reaches peaceful political speech and uses criminal penalties too readily

**Labels:** Broadness, Necessity, Proportionality.

The reports argue that the undefined term places a broad swath of non-violent political speech inside the offense, so the law is not the least intrusive instrument, and that criminal penalties should be reserved for grave speech crimes while Article 495 also reaches minute disruptions of public order. Each report concludes that Article 495 violates ICCPR Article 19. Sokhet, printed pp. 20–21. Sovannchhay, printed p. 38. Kong Raiya, printed p. 23.

Sokhet's conclusion, printed p. 24, argues that "disrupt social order" is so broad and vague that prosecutors and judges can call any action disruptive, and that Cambodia must revise Article 495.

### A3. These prosecutions were not shown to be necessary or proportionate

**Labels:** Necessity, Proportionality.

Each report then applies General Comment No. 34, paragraph 35: the state must show the precise nature of the threat and a direct and immediate connection between the expression and that threat.

- **Kong Raiya.** The report argues that the state did not show a precise threat or a direct and immediate connection, and that the same shirts had been sold in 2017 without unrest. Printed pp. 24–25. A separate argument, printed p. 25, says the 29 November Facebook ban on political use covered all political discourse without exception (**Broadness, Necessity, Proportionality**).
- **Ros Sokhet.** The report argues that Phnom Penh Municipal Police Chief Sar Thet described the crime as "incitement to provoke social insecurity by criticizing the top leader," which the report treats as targeting criticism, and that even if public order was the aim, the prosecution named no disorder, no person who might have been incited, and no direct and immediate connection. The police witness said the department had received no complaints. Printed pp. 21–23. The report's sentence is that "his criminal prosecution was neither necessary nor proportionate."
- **Kak Sovannchhay.** The speech the report treats as criticism is a Facebook caption, "the traitor, family of the traitor, children of the traitor," and Telegram descriptions of Hun Sen as "the stupid leader" and "blind man." The report argues that the prosecution never specified the social disorder, who might have been incited, or the threat to an official, and did not explain a direct and immediate connection or why criminal proceedings were necessary and proportionate. Printed pp. 39–40.

### A4. Points that belong to one case only

**Ros Sokhet, de facto defamation. Labels: Proportionality.** Printed p. 23. The report argues that Article 495 was applied so as to prohibit statements about the Prime Minister that were perceived as defamatory, and that even if the posts were defamatory, an 18-month prison sentence was disproportionate. It cites General Comment No. 34, paragraph 47: imprisonment is never an appropriate penalty for defamation.

**Kak Sovannchhay, Article 502. Label: `unmapped—review required`.** Printed p. 39. The report argues that criminalizing insult of a public official violates Article 19 on its face, because "the mere fact that forms of expression are considered to be insulting to a public figure is not sufficient to justify the imposition of penalties" (General Comment No. 34, paras. 11 and 38). That is a facial claim about insult, not the Article 495 vagueness paragraph. A reviewer may also attach Proportionality. Do not force it into Cluster A1.

**Kak Sovannchhay, two Telegram snippets. Labels: Necessity, Proportionality. Keep this separate from A3.** Printed pp. 41–42. The report says a 4 June clip ("drive away, defeat the Vietnamese invaders") and a 23 June clip ("I will hit you on your head") could be read, in the light most favorable to the prosecution, as advocating hatred or inciting violence. It argues they still should not have been prosecuted: the second was a private exchange with no national, racial, or religious target; the first was not shown to create a reasonable probability of imminent harm, was aimed at one member of a private group, and came from a child with autism who had little power over that audience. The Rabat Plan is the authority. Do not fold these clips into the criticism argument.

**Fair trial.** Sovannchhay's report also argues that refusing a medical expert violated ICCPR Article 14(3)(e). That stays `unmapped—review required`.

## Cluster B — Poland, Criminal Code Article 196

**Case:** Poland v. Elżbieta Podleśna, Anna Prus, and Joanna Gzyra-Iskandar (November 2021).

**Who argues:** Lisa Davis is the named author and a member of the TrialWatch Experts Panel. ABA Center staff helped draft the report. The grade of D is Davis's. Treat the legal analysis as jointly attributed to Davis and the ABA partner, not as an institutional Clooney Foundation determination.

Article 196 covers whoever offends the religious feelings of other persons by publicly insulting an object of religious worship or a place designated for public religious ceremonies, with a penalty of up to two years.

### B1. "Offend religious feelings" is not precise enough

**Labels:** Legality, Vagueness. Printed p. 23.

The report argues that offending religious feelings is subjective, that the law gives no guidance on degree, on who counts as offended, or on religious feelings as against general sensibilities, and that this leaves charging discretion unfettered.

### B2. Protecting feelings is not a listed aim

**Label: `unmapped—review required`.** Printed pp. 23–24.

The report argues that Article 196 fails the legitimacy requirement of ICCPR Article 19(3) and the European Convention, because anti-blasphemy laws protect ideas from ridicule rather than individuals from violence or discrimination. It cites General Comment No. 34, paragraph 48, and the Special Rapporteurs on expression and on religion. Legitimate aim is not one of the five headline labels. Do not rename this as Vagueness.

### B3. The offense is broad, and two years is too severe for this speech

**Labels:** Broadness, Necessity, Proportionality. Printed p. 24.

The report argues that the imprecision of "offend religious feelings" covers a broad swath of speech, including routine LGBTIQ+ activism, so the law is not the least intrusive instrument, and that up to two years' imprisonment is not reserved for grave speech crimes. It concludes that Article 196 violates ICCPR Article 19 and ECHR Article 10.

### B4. This prosecution was aimed at the rainbow, not only at the sacred image

**Labels:** Necessity, Proportionality, plus the same unmapped legitimacy point. Printed pp. 25–28.

The report argues that the posters of the Virgin Mary with a rainbow halo were protected expression, that Article 196 fails the precision test, and that protecting "subjective feelings of offensiveness" is not a legitimate aim. It argues that "protection of morals" does not cover prejudice against a protected group, citing *Bayev and others v. Russia*. It says prosecution evidence, including priest Tadeusz Łebkowski and auxiliary prosecutor Kaja Godek, objected to the LGBTIQ+ meaning of the colors. It then argues that, even assuming protection of morals, the prosecution showed no incitement to religious hatred or violence. It quotes the trial court: the defendants' actions "did not constitute a sign of hate speech, did not incite violence, [and] did not intimidate other people." The only harm described is that some people felt shocked, saddened, or hurt. The report argues that a criminal prosecution gave absolute primacy to those feelings.

**Discrimination** under ICCPR Articles 2 and 26 and ECHR Article 14 is a further claim in the same report. It stays `unmapped—review required`.

### What the report says had already happened

The District Court of Płock acquitted all three on 2 March 2021. The prosecution appealed. This report was written for release before the appeal hearing then scheduled for 10 November. The appeal result is not in the report. The acquittal is the court's decision, described by a later report. It is not acceptance of this report's argument. The grade remains D. The report says the prosecution caused significant harm even though the court acquitted.

## Cluster C — Nigeria, offensive publication and the #EndSARS cases

**Case:** Nigeria v. Eromosele Adene and Nicholas Mbah (April 2022). These are two proceedings. Count them as two cases when a comparison needs a case total, and do not double-count a shared argument as two arguments about one case.

**Who argues:** ABA Center staff drafted the report. Michael Hamilton, TrialWatch Experts Panel, assigned the grade of D. The expression analysis is the partner draft. The grade is Hamilton's fairness assessment.

### C1. "Offensive publication" is not precise enough, and it covers protected speech

**Labels:** Legality, Vagueness. Printed pp. 33–34. This is argued for Adene's charge.

The report argues that offensiveness is subjective, that the law does not say what degree of offense merits charges or who must be offended, and that the resulting discretion is ripe for abuse. It also argues that expression regarded as deeply offensive remains protected (General Comment No. 34, para. 11), so the offense contravenes Article 19 on its face and as applied.

### C2. Adene's prosecution was not shown to be necessary or proportionate

**Labels:** Necessity, Proportionality. Printed pp. 35–36.

The charge sheet language the report quotes is "ENDSARS MARCH AGAINST POLICE BRUTALITY AND EXTRAL [sic] JUDICIAL MURDER OF OUR PROTESTING COLLEAGUES." The report argues that political speech about SARS abuses merited maximum protection. It argues that even if public order or national security was the aim, the state did not show a precise threat or a direct and immediate connection. It says the prosecution presented no evidence that the call for protests endangered the peace, and that the Yaba Magistrate Court struck out the case for want of prosecution. It argues that criminal proceedings with a custodial penalty were not the least intrusive instrument, and that the speech was not alleged to meet the grave-crime threshold. It cites the African Court's *Konaté* judgment for the rule that custodial sentences for speech are confined to exceptional cases.

### C3. Mbah's expression claim is stated, then deferred

**Labels:** Necessity, Proportionality, with the aim point left `unmapped—review required`. Printed p. 36.

The report says Mbah's arrest, detention, and prosecution "appear" to have been aimed at suppressing peaceful criticism, "in violation of the requirement that restrictions on freedom of expression possess a legitimate objective and be necessary and proportional," and that this "will be discussed at length in the following sections." Those sections are about assembly and abuse of process. Do not invent a separate Mbah expression passage that the report did not write. Do not treat the Adene offensive-publication analysis as if it were also pleaded against Mbah. Mbah's charges, as the report states them, were unlawful assembly, breaking curfew, and attempting to set fire to the Ojo Local Government Secretariat.

### C4. Assembly is a different right

**Label: `unmapped—review required`.** Printed pp. 37–39.

The report argues that both men were prosecuted for promoting or joining peaceful assemblies, that no evidence of violent intent was presented, and that states should not use a vague definition of "public order" to justify overbroad restrictions on assembly (General Comment No. 37, para. 44). Peaceful includes conduct that annoys or temporarily obstructs. That is an assembly argument. It uses some of the same words as the expression labels. It is not Cluster C1.

Abuse of process and improper political motive, printed pp. 40–43, also stay unmapped.

### What the report says had already happened

Adene spent nine days in jail and was released on bail. In October 2021 the Yaba Magistrate Court struck the case out for want of diligent prosecution. That is a procedural ending, not a holding that the offense was vague or the penalty disproportionate.

Mbah was arrested on 29 October 2020, held for months, and released on bail on 15 June after eight months. As of this April 2022 report, the Yaba case was still pending and related charges had been filed in the Lagos High Court. "Pending" here is the report's statement. It is not a current status, and it is not a finding that he remains detained.

## Cluster D — Hong Kong, Crimes Ordinance sedition

**Case:** Hong Kong SAR v. Tam Tak-chi (May 2022).

**Who argues:** Elizabeth Wilmshurst, a member of the TrialWatch Expert Panel, is the named author. The legal assessment is hers, not necessarily the Clooney Foundation's. This is not an ABA-drafted report. She assigned the grade of D.

This is its own cluster. It uses the same three-part test, but the statute, the national-security framing, and the foreseeability point are not the Cambodia Article 495 boilerplate.

Section 10 criminalizes uttering seditious words. Section 9 defines seditious intention through a list that includes bringing the government into hatred or contempt, exciting disaffection, raising discontent, promoting ill-will between classes, inciting violence, and counselling disobedience.

### D1. The sedition definitions are vague, and this application was not foreseeable

**Labels:** Legality, Vagueness, Broadness. Printed pp. 40–43.

The report argues that the colonial ordinance is itself too broad, and that the court applied it to political statements protected by human rights law. It says the court dismissed the defense vagueness concern by treating words such as hostility, ill will, disaffection, contempt, and hatred as flexible with the times, and then did not say, charge by charge, which part of section 9 was violated. It argues that slogans including "Liberate Hong Kong, revolution of our times" and "Five Demands," criticism of the police, the Communist Party, and the National Security Law, and campaign lines asking people to vote, were all treated as seditious. It argues that this application reaches targets the ordinance does not name, and that a person could not have known these phrases were seditious. It argues the prosecution was not foreseeable: this was the first sedition trial in decades, and the Tong Ying-kit precedent treating "Liberate Hong Kong" as a national-security offense came after most of the speech in this case.

### D2. National security was not shown, and 21 months is too severe

**Labels:** Necessity, Proportionality. Printed pp. 44–47.

The report argues that criticism of government, parties, and the National Security Law is political speech entitled to more protection, not less (General Comment No. 34, para. 38). It argues that a national-security limit must identify a specific threat and a direct and immediate connection (para. 35), and, under the Johannesburg Principles, must show intent to incite imminent violence, likelihood, and a direct connection. It says the court acknowledged that no violence was instigated, that the authorities presented no evidence of violence or imminent harm on the dates in question, and that sedition was not charged until September 2020 although an arrest had occurred in January. It argues that the verdict's "incitement" is incitement to vote, which is political speech and cannot be a necessary restriction. It argues that even taking the prosecution's case at its highest, imprisonment was not justified, and that 21 months for uttering seditious words was too severe for speeches that created no imminent risk of harm, were not aimed at a protected group under the Rabat Plan, and included protected political speech.

The report's separate claims about a designated national-security judge, equality before the courts, and improper political motive stay `unmapped—review required`.

### What the report says had already happened

Tam Tak-chi was denied bail and detained through a delayed trial. He was convicted in March 2022. On 20 April 2022 he was sentenced to 40 months and a HK$5,000 fine. Of that, the sedition counts drew 21 months, of which 12 were consecutive. The longest single count was two years for inciting an unauthorized assembly. The May 2022 report cannot explain the conviction or the sentence. The court's rejection of the defense vagueness argument is the court's position, as described by the report, not reception of this report.

## What the clusters are, and what they are not

| Cluster | Statute | Cases | Shared labels | Held apart |

| B | Poland Article 196 | 1 (3 defendants) | Legality, Vagueness, Broadness, Necessity, Proportionality | Legitimate-aim failure; discrimination |
| C | Nigeria offensive publication, as applied to Adene | 2 proceedings | Legality, Vagueness, Necessity, Proportionality for Adene | Mbah has no matching expression passage; assembly stays unmapped |

Count seven proceedings if Adene and Mbah are split, or six reports. Do not count each label as another case. Do not average the D grades. Do not describe any of these arguments as successful.

The repeated sentences — precision and unfettered discretion, least intrusive instrument, grave speech crimes only, precise threat and direct connection, the Rabat factors — are a template across the ABA-drafted reports and, in part, the Wilmshurst report. That template is a lead for the next PDF. It is not a finding that the argument was received.

Later outcomes still need a source other than these reports. A news sentence can be stored only as a proposed lead, beside its link, after a person confirms the full defendant name and the event date.
